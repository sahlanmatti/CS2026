B)

I chose Simon Willisons blog post from 2023, titled: “What’s the worst that can happen?”. Even though it’s written three years ago, the subject is very relevant.

  • The article is about the critical AI security flaw known as “Prompt Injection”. In prompt injection the goal of the attack is to trick a LLM to bypass its original instructions and do what the attacker then prompts it to do.
  • Willison demonstrates in his article how an attacker can use prompt injection for data exfiltration, forcing the AI to send the user's private data to the attackers server.
  • What Willison makes clear, is that filtering and limiting are not enough. There is currently no way to be fully protected against prompt injection without reducing AI’s capabilities significantly.
  • My question: Is it safe to give an AI agent a read/write-access when there is any private information handled? For example emails, banking or calendars.

C)

In my h1 assignment, I reviewed the paper 'A Review on Penetration Testing for Privacy of Deep Learning Models' (2026). The paper highlighted that traditional penetration testing frameworks are completely insufficient for testing the unique vulnerabilities of AI models and that there is no single method to protect them yet. This links perfectly to Simon Willison’s blog about Prompt Injection. Willison’s real life examples of stealing data and tricking the AI prove the main point of my H1 assignment. Hacking AI is completely different from hacking normal software. And because we can’t fully patch these prompt injections yet, we need those new, specialized AI penetration testing frameworks to understand the exposure before giving AI tools access to critical data.
